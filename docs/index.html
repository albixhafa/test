          How to read this

          Money doesn't vanish in a selloff

          Every share sold has a buyer, so trading alone does not move money between markets. What does is investors putting new money into funds or pulling it out, and where they park it. That is what this page measures, alongside prices, futures positioning and the cash piles that money can come from.

          Real fund flows

          Every State Street SPDR fund (the S&P 500's SPY, the eleven sector funds, gold's GLD, T-bills, Treasuries, corporate and high-yield bonds, and international stocks) publishes its shares outstanding every day. When investors put money in, new shares are created; when they pull it out, shares are cancelled. New shares times the share price is the day's flow, in real dollars. Crypto's flow is the change in the total supply of dollar stablecoins, the cash that moves on and off crypto rails. SPDR funds are a large, consistent slice of US ETFs (well over a trillion dollars), not the whole market, so read sizes as a sample and directions as the signal. Stablecoins, by contrast, are counted in full, so over long ranges crypto looks large next to the other classes; compare each class with its own past. Markets without an SPDR fund (Nasdaq 100, small caps, bitcoin, the dollar, broad commodities) are tracked for price only.

          The liquidity wheel

          Each fund's flow is added up within its class, so rotation inside a class (from tech into utilities, say) cancels out and only money crossing between classes remains. The Sidelines balance the wheel: money that came into the tracked funds from outside (bank deposits, other funds) is shown leaving the sidelines, and money that left them altogether is shown going to the sidelines. Between classes, each net outflow is split across net inflows in proportion to size: a way to read the rotation, not a ledger of who bought from whom.

          Flows vs returns, and how unusual a flow is

          The quadrant chart puts each fund's return across and its net flow (as a share of the fund) up. Top right is money chasing strength, top left buying the dip, bottom right taking profits, bottom left capitulation. "How unusual" ranks the fund's flow over the selected range against every window of the same length in the last three years: the 99th percentile means only 1% of such windows saw bigger inflows. SPY is the fund traders use for hedging and quick moves, so its flows swing far more than those of buy-and-hold S&P 500 funds; read it as tactical money.

          Positioning

          The CFTC publishes, every Friday for the previous Tuesday, how many futures contracts each type of trader holds. For stock, bond, dollar and bitcoin futures the page shows leveraged funds (hedge funds and CTAs); for gold and crude oil, managed money. When their net position is at the top or bottom of its three-year range, the trade is crowded: there are few buyers (or sellers) left, and a surprise can force a fast unwind. One exception: in Treasury and bitcoin futures, hedge funds' net short is mostly the basis trade (owning the bond or the coin and selling the future against it), not a bet on prices falling, so an extreme there says more about that trade than about direction.

          Rates and credit

          • 3-month T-bill yield: what cash earns. The higher it is, the more it pays to wait on the sidelines.
          • 10-year Treasury yield: what the US government pays to borrow for ten years, the anchor for most asset prices.
          • Yield curve: the 10-year yield minus the 2-year. Below zero (inverted) has preceded most US recessions.
          • Real yield: the 10-year yield after expected inflation (TIPS). High real yields compete with gold and stocks.
          • High-yield spread: the extra yield risky companies pay over Treasuries. It widens when lenders get nervous.
          • Financial conditions: the Chicago Fed's index of credit, leverage and risk. Above zero is tighter than average.

          Cash buckets

          • Stablecoins: dollars waiting on crypto rails. Rising supply is often new buying power for crypto.
          • All money market funds: every US money fund, retail and institutional (OFR, monthly). The biggest pool of sidelined cash; its net flows are the change in assets minus the interest earned at the T-bill rate. The wheel shows them next to the sidelines for comparison.
          • Retail money funds: where households park cash. Jumps during selloffs show de-risking.
          • Overnight reverse repo: excess cash money funds lend to the Fed.
          • Treasury General Account: the government's cash at the Fed. A rising TGA drains liquidity from markets.
          • Fed net liquidity: Fed balance sheet minus TGA minus reverse repo, a popular gauge of liquidity available to markets.

          Date range

          The range bar under the menu (1W to All, or Custom dates) applies to every chart, table and the changes shown on the headline tiles. Ranges inside the last three years use daily data; longer ones use monthly totals. The "Right now" summary and the outlook cards always describe the present and the full history, whatever range is selected.

          Inflation-adjusted view

          Tick Inflation-adjusted to restate every dollar amount in today's dollars and every price change as a real return, using the US consumer price index (CPI). CPI is monthly and published with a lag of about a month, so short recent ranges barely change. Correlations and the risk score are unaffected, and the "Right now" summary and the outlook stay in plain dollars.

          History and replay

          The history chart shows the same class-to-class flows week by week for three years, or month by month since 2017. Click any bar, pick a past selloff from the wheel's menu, or click a row in the selloff heatmap to replay that period in the wheel.

          Outlook

          The outlook cards summarise patterns: which markets held up or bounced back in past selloffs, where money has kept flowing in or out, and where price and flow disagree. They describe history and current flows. They are not forecasts or advice.

          Selloffs

          A selloff is any fall of at least 7% in the S&P 500 from a swing high, confirmed once it rebounds by the same amount. The one still in progress, if any, is marked as ongoing and left out of the playbook medians.
