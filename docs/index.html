          How to read this

          Money doesn't vanish in a selloff

          Every share sold has a buyer, so a falling market does not "drain" a fixed pot of cash. What changes is who holds what and at what price. The useful question is where sellers park their proceeds and which markets see persistent buying while stocks fall. That shows up as relative strength, heavy turnover and rising cash balances, which is what this page tracks.

          The liquidity wheel

          Each market's estimated flow is added up within its class, so rotation inside a class (from tech into utilities, say) cancels out and only money crossing between classes remains. Cash balances the wheel: if more was sold than bought across the tracked markets, the difference is shown flowing into cash, and if more was bought, it is shown coming out of cash.

          Estimated flows

          Real fund flows (ETF creations and redemptions, money fund subscriptions) mostly sit behind paid data. Flows here are estimated with Chaikin money flow: each day's dollar volume is signed by where the close landed in the day's range. Each market is then compared with its own average over the previous year, so only unusual buying or selling counts; without that, rising markets would look like they attract money every single day. The rotation map then splits each net outflow across net inflows in proportion to size; any imbalance is routed to or from Cash & sidelines. Read it as a picture of pressure, not a ledger of who bought from whom.

          Cash buckets

          • Stablecoins: dollars waiting on crypto rails. Rising supply is often new buying power for crypto.
          • Retail money funds: where households park cash. Jumps during selloffs show de-risking.
          • Overnight reverse repo: excess cash money funds lend to the Fed.
          • Treasury General Account: the government's cash at the Fed. A rising TGA drains liquidity from markets.
          • Fed net liquidity: Fed balance sheet minus TGA minus reverse repo, a popular gauge of liquidity available to markets.

          History and replay

          The history chart shows the same class-to-class flows week by week for three years, or month by month since 2017. Click any bar, pick a past selloff from the wheel's menu, or click a row in the selloff heatmap to replay that period in the wheel.

          Outlook

          The outlook cards summarise patterns: which markets held up or bounced back in past selloffs, where unusual buying or selling has persisted, and where price and flow disagree. They describe history and current pressure. They are not forecasts or advice.

          Selloffs

          A selloff is any fall of at least 7% in the S&P 500 from a swing high, confirmed once it rebounds by the same amount. The one still in progress, if any, is marked as ongoing and left out of the playbook medians.

          Not investment advice. Data can be delayed, revised or wrong.
